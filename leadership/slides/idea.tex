%% 1. This paper
\begin{frame}{This paper}
\vspace{0.05cm}
\small
School principals are an input in the education production function: value-added estimates put a one standard deviation better principal at 0.05 to 0.10 SD of student achievement. Much less is known about the production process itself: how leadership translates into teacher behavior, classroom organization, and family engagement, and through them into learning.
\vspace{0.35cm}

We study this process using mandatory principal turnover in Madrid's public primary schools. Two institutional rules, compulsory retirement at 65 and a twelve-year limit on tenure, generate leadership transitions whose timing is unrelated to school performance. Linked administrative and survey data on every principal, teacher, classroom, pupil, and parent let us estimate the effect of a leadership change on each intermediate outcome, not only on test scores.
\vspace{0.35cm}

Teacher practice, wellbeing, and collaboration, classroom climate, and parental engagement are treated as outcomes in their own right, and as the channels through which leadership reaches achievement.
\end{frame}

%% 2. What we know
\begin{frame}{What we know}
\vspace{0.1cm}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.1cm}L{9.9cm}@{}}
\lab{Leaders matter} & A one SD better principal raises achievement by 0.05 to 0.10 SD \cit{Branch, Hanushek, and Rivkin, 2012; B\"ohlmark, Gr\"onqvist, and Vlachos, 2016}. The effect is comparable to a teacher's, over a whole school \cit{Grissom, Egalite, and Lindsay, 2021}. \\[0.3em]
\lab{Changes matter} & When the principal changes, achievement falls by about 0.03 SD the next year and teacher turnover rises \cit{Bartanen, Grissom, and Rogers, 2019}. \\[0.3em]
\lab{Leadership and the school} & Where teachers value their principal's instructional and relational leadership, schools show better climate, more collaboration, better classroom management, higher wellbeing and job satisfaction \cit{OECD, TALIS 2018}. Correlational. \\
\end{tabular}
\end{frame}

%% 2b. What has been opened
\begin{frame}{What has been opened, and what has not}
\vspace{0.1cm}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.1cm}L{9.9cm}@{}}
\lab{Personnel} & Effective principals keep their best teachers and lose their weakest \cit{Grissom and Bartanen, 2019, AERJ}. Principals reshape the staff through hiring and retention \cit{Bartanen and Grissom, 2023, JHR}. Who teaches in the school is the channel the literature has identified. \\[0.3em]
\lab{Procedures} & For superintendents, better leaders change school priorities, working procedures and violence \cit{Lavy, Boiko, and Rachkovski, 2023, EJ}. \\[0.3em]
\lab{Not yet seen} & Under a change of leader the school did not choose: what the same teachers do and how they feel, how classrooms are run, and how families respond. The inside of the school. \\
\end{tabular}
\vspace{0.4cm}

\muted{\footnotesize The literature shows that leaders matter and that they choose who teaches. This paper is about what they do with the teachers they have.}
\end{frame}

%% 3. The richness
\begin{frame}{Intermediate outcomes: what we observe at the school level}
\vspace{0.05cm}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.1cm}L{9.9cm}@{}}
\lab{Teachers} & Reported classroom practice, collaboration, participation in training, self-efficacy, and wellbeing. \\[0.3em]
\lab{Classrooms} & Allocation of teachers and support hours across groups, disciplinary climate, and pupils' reports of safety and bullying. \\[0.3em]
\lab{Families} & Involvement in the school, satisfaction with teachers and the centre, and perceptions of school quality. \\[0.3em]
\lab{Pupils} & Engagement, perceptions of the school, and standardized achievement by subject. \\
\end{tabular}
\vspace{0.4cm}

Each domain is measured for every school and year. Teacher and family outcomes enter the analysis as dependent variables, and jointly as mediators of the effect on achievement.
\end{frame}

%% 5. Why Madrid
\begin{frame}{Setting}
\vspace{0.05cm}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.1cm}L{9.9cm}@{}}
\lab{Institutions} & Spanish principals are teachers selected from the staff for four-year terms. Staffing, pay, and curriculum are set by the regional administration. A change of principal therefore changes leadership and organization while holding the teaching staff fixed, which separates the leadership effect from the personnel channel identified in the US literature. \\[0.3em]
\lab{Exogenous timing} & Terms end by compulsory retirement at 65 and by a limit of twelve years in office, both determined years in advance. Transitions induced by these rules are orthogonal to contemporaneous school shocks. \\[0.3em]
\lab{Successor characteristics} & Incoming principals come from the same staff and are observed as classroom teachers in the year before appointment, which provides pre-determined measures of their type. \\[0.3em]
\lab{Data} & Universe of public primary schools in Madrid, 2015 to 2018: principal and teacher questionnaires, timetables, and census surveys and standardized tests of all pupils and parents by classroom. \\
\end{tabular}
\end{frame}

\lab{Treated} & Schools whose clock runs out in 2016, 2017, or 2018: a new leader arrives for reasons unrelated to the school's trajectory. \\[0.2em]
\lab{Comparison} & Schools whose clock runs out later. Staggered event study on the calendar; 2SLS for the realized change. \\[0.2em]
\lab{Benchmark} & Exits that are not rule-based (resignations, non-renewals), kept separate: they carry the selection the literature worries about \cit{Miller, 2013; Bartanen, Grissom, and Rogers, 2019}. \\[0.2em]
\lab{Heterogeneity} & By the successor's pre-appointment practices and wellbeing, the selection committee's score of their project, and the staff's assessment of the outgoing principal. \\

%% 8. Three parameters
\begin{frame}{Estimands}
\vspace{0.05cm}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{3.1cm}L{9.9cm}@{}}
\lab{Average effect} & The effect of a mandatory leadership transition on each domain: teachers, classrooms, families, achievement. \\[0.4em]
\lab{Variance of leader effects} & If leaders differ, the dispersion of outcome changes is larger around rule-based transitions than in non-transition years \cit{Jones and Olken, 2005}. Identified without classifying leaders. \\[0.4em]
\lab{Heterogeneity} & Treatment effects by pre-determined characteristics of the incoming and outgoing principal: prior classroom practice, project score, staff assessment. \\
\end{tabular}
\vspace{0.4cm}

\muted{\footnotesize The three together identify whether leadership matters, how much leaders differ, and which observable characteristics predict effective leadership.}
\end{frame}

%% 10. Open questions
\begin{frame}{Open questions}
\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.9em}
\item \textbf{Mechanisms to prioritize.} Which intermediate outcomes should respond within the first year of a new principal, and which later?
\item \textbf{What the data must have.} A stable teacher identifier across years; the principal's age and years in office; the survey items that rate the principal.
\item \textbf{Sample of rule-based transitions} in 2015 to 2018, and access to appointment records and later years from the Consejer\'ia.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
